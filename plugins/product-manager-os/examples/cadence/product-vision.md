# Product Vision — Cadence

## The one-paragraph version
By 2030, no engineering team holds a meeting to find out what happened. The written record of the work *is* the status, and the questions a manager used to ask out loud — who's stuck, what slipped, what needs a decision — are answered before they're asked. Cadence is where that record lives and where those answers surface.

## A day in 2030
Dana runs platform engineering at a 90-person company. Eleven reports across four time zones.

She used to start Monday at 9:00 with a 30-minute all-hands standup that three people slept through and two joined from a car. She killed it in 2026 and got the half-hour back, then quietly lost it again reading eleven check-ins every morning looking for the one that mattered.

In 2030 she opens Cadence at 8:40 with coffee. The screen has four things on it. Marco has been waiting on staging credentials for three days — not because he escalated, but because Cadence noticed the same sentence three mornings running. The mobile release slipped two days, and the two downstream people who need to know have already been told. A decision about the auth migration has been sitting unmade for a week and is now blocking two workstreams. Everything else is fine, and the screen says so rather than making her verify it.

She spends six minutes there. She replies to Marco directly, reassigns nothing, and books a fifteen-minute call on the auth decision with exactly the three people who need to be in it.

At 9:00, where the standup used to be, she does no status work at all. The thing she no longer thinks about is whether she's missed something. That anxiety — the low background hum of *what don't I know* — is the thing that actually left, and it left because the record is complete and something reads it properly.

Her reports notice something different: nobody asks them for an update. They write four sentences when they finish a thing, in the tool they're already in, and that's the whole obligation. The junior engineer who joined in March has never been in a standup and finds the concept strange when it comes up.

## What we believe (the load-bearing bets)
| # | Belief | Why we hold it | What would falsify it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Synchronous status meetings are a workaround for an unreadable written record, not a human need | Teams that adopt async check-ins and *keep* them (survive week 2) don't ask for the meeting back — 94% of surviving teams | Teams reinstating standups while check-in volume stays high |
| 2 | The bottleneck moves from writing to reading | Managers already open the digest 71% of days but spend 40 seconds in it | Managers reading thoroughly and still missing nothing |
| 3 | Enough signal exists in ordinary check-in text to answer a manager's real questions | Hand-labelling found detectable blockers in 61% of stalled work, days before escalation | Detection accuracy plateauing below usefulness after real feedback |
| 4 | Distributed work keeps growing as a share of engineering | Sustained across three years of our own signups | A durable reversal to co-located default |

## Who is better off
- **The engineering manager** — stops being the human aggregation layer. Gets back the attention, not just the half hour.
- **The engineer** — writes four sentences and is never interrupted for a verbal update.
- **The person two teams away** — finds out about the slip that affects them without anyone remembering to tell them.
- **Not served by this:** teams under five people who are already in one room. They should not buy Cadence, and we should stop marketing to them.

## The boundary — what this is never
- **A project management tool.** We never own the plan, the tickets, or the deadline. The moment we do, writing a check-in becomes reporting to a system of record and people start gaming it.
- **A productivity surveillance product.** We will not ship individual output scoring, activity ranking, or anything a manager could use to compare two people. This is permanent, and it's the line that makes people willing to write honestly.
- **A chat tool.** Slack won. We attach to it.

## Where we are today
We solved the writing half and created the reading problem. 4,000 teams write check-ins; 1,420 do it on three or more days a week. 39% of teams don't survive to a second qualifying week, and our own research says the reason is that nobody visibly reads what they wrote. We are a good input mechanism attached to an unread output. The AI Blocker Detector is the first honest attempt at the reading half, and it's one of maybe six things that have to work.

## Horizons
| Horizon | What becomes true | Not yet |
|---|---|---|
| Now | The record is complete and easy to write | Nobody reliably reads it |
| Next | The manager's recurring questions are answered without being asked — blockers, slips, stalled decisions surface on their own | Cross-team awareness |
| Eventually | The organization's state is legible without anyone assembling it, and the meeting to find out never gets scheduled | — |

## Decision test
- **Blocker detection vs. a nicer check-in editor** → detection. The writing half is solved; the vision says the constraint is reading. This one was genuinely contested in planning and the vision settled it in a sentence.
- **Individual streak leaderboards vs. team-level digest quality** → digest quality. Leaderboards cross the surveillance boundary, which is permanent — so it isn't a prioritization question at all.
